Root Cause

What 25 years of Test cricket looks like through Wins Above Replacement

Basketball has Wins Above Replacement. Test cricket doesn't, so I built one from 25 years of ball-by-ball data.

Coverage 2001–2026 Matches 890 Deliveries 1,715,773 Source Cricsheet

In short

  • WAR (Wins Above Replacement) is one number for how many extra wins a player earned their team, above what a replacement-level player would have earned in the same matches. Basketball and baseball have had it for years; Test cricket hasn't, until now.
  • Built it from 1.72 million ball-by-ball deliveries, adjusted for opposition, conditions, and how much each moment mattered.
  • Joe Root comes out on top, worth roughly 16 extra wins to England across his career, more than anyone else since 2001.
  • James Anderson comes second, and like Root he had a long, successful career for a team that plays more Tests than almost anyone. Nathan Lyon rates third, which suggests he's underrated, and will surprise a lot of people. More on why below.

WAR (Wins Above Replacement) is the closest thing basketball and baseball have to a single number that answers "how good is this player, really?" It doesn't ask how many points or runs someone produced. It asks how many wins they added for their team, above what a freely-available replacement-level player would have added in the same games. Batting average and bowling average get you partway there, but they don't know who you were facing, what the pitch was doing, or whether the moment mattered.

I built a metric to measure it for Test cricket, and I keep testing and revising it. Everything below comes from the model as it stands today. The companion piece, Measure Twice, covers how it was built and tested.

The idea, in plain terms

Imagine every Test team keeps a replacement player in reserve. Not a star, just a solid domestic pro who'd slot in if someone got dropped. WAR asks how many extra wins a player earned their team over that replacement, in the same matches.

The rest is about defining “the same matches” carefully.

  • Who you faced. A hundred against a strong attack should count for more than a hundred against a weak one.
  • What the pitch was doing. Runs on a green seamer are harder to come by than runs on a flat road, and wickets on a raging turner are partly the surface's doing, not just the bowler.
  • What a wicket is worth. Not a textbook bowling average, but the measured effect that losing a batter has on a team's chances of winning.
  • Whether the moment mattered. A wicket that breaks a nervy run chase is worth more than the same wicket in a match already decided.
  • Turning all of that into wins, using the historical relationship between how big a team's run advantage was and how often that produced a win.

Is the data any good?

Everything here comes from Cricsheet, a free ball-by-ball archive of international cricket. Coverage starts in 2001 and runs to the present. The raw archive holds 914 matches, but 24 are women’s Tests, so the analysis uses the 890 men’s Tests. That is 1.72 million deliveries, each with the batter, the bowler, the runs and the dismissal if there was one.

Before trusting any of it, I checked it against known history. Alastair Cook’s career is closed, so his total is fixed. Summing his raw runs from the ball-by-ball data gave exactly his real tally of 12,472. The dismissal mix was right too, 59.2% caught, 17.5% bowled and 16.5% lbw, with the rest split across run outs, stumpings and oddities. The shift in trends across eras shows up in the numbers as well. The draw rate fell from 22.5% of matches in 2000–04 to 7.4% in 2025–26, partly because of the shift toward more attacking Test cricket and partly because of the World Test Championship, where bilateral series now feed a points table.

Across everyone in the dataset, not just the top 20, batting has generated about 2.0 times as much value as bowling. That split comes from how a wicket is priced. The top three are closer than the table suggests. That is the career picture. Ask instead who was most valuable every time they played and the answer changes. Root drops to fifteenth, and Pat Cummins leads on a per-match basis despite a much shorter career.

Warne's 2005 tops the table. It spans 90 wickets across the calendar year, over series against Pakistan, New Zealand, England, the West Indies and South Africa, including the five-Test Ashes series Australia lost 2–1 despite his form. Six of the matches behind that total draw on the scorecard layer rather than ball-by-ball data. It's part of a run of five spin- and pace-heavy seasons that fill most of the top of the list. All five sit in 2001–2008, the window where coverage is patchier and the replacement level leans on the scorecard layer rather than pure ball-by-ball data (marked † where the season itself draws on it directly). Bumrah's 2024 is the best season since coverage became complete in 2009, the highest total behind full ball-by-ball data with no coarser layer underneath it anywhere in the chain.

He took 71 wickets at an average of 14.93 in 13 Tests. The year started in South Africa, ran through home series against England, Bangladesh and New Zealand, and ended in Australia for the Border-Gavaskar series. The New Zealand series was India's 0–3 home whitewash, in which most of India's other bowlers had miserable figures. Opposition quality across those 13 Tests averaged almost exactly the dataset norm (0.999 against 1.0), and leverage was fractionally below average (0.953×); it wasn't a soft year against weak sides in high-stakes moments, his best output came in ordinary conditions. The one dip came in Pune against New Zealand, in the second Test of the whitewash: wicketless for 57 runs, a bowling value of −37.6 runs above replacement and the only negative match of his season.

Michael Clarke's 2012 doesn't make the list. It ranks #32 overall, despite being one of the most dominant years in the sport's history by reputation, with 1,595 runs at an official average of 106.33 and four double-centuries in a calendar year, one of them an unbeaten 329 against India. The dataset reproduces the runs, the double-centuries and the 329 exactly. It isn't a lack of runs or soft conditions, since the bowling he faced and the pitches he batted on were close to average difficulty. Two things explain the gap. A lot of his runs came in matches that were already decided or heading for a draw, so they counted for less than the same runs in a tight game. And it took more runs to buy a win in 2010–14 than it does in recent seasons, so the same runs earn more WAR today.

Every era of Test cricket has its pub arguments. A few of this one's biggest can be tested against the numbers, and few come out as tidily as either side would like.

Lyon third on the overall leaderboard, above every specialist batter bar Root and within a win of Anderson, may come as a surprise. He has never been talked about alongside Warne or Murali the way this model places him. His reputation is a very good, high-volume off-spinner, not necessarily an all-time great. A 25-person expert panel ranking the greatest cricketers of 2000 to 2025 for ESPN placed Ashwin 25th and left Lyon off the list entirely, the same gap this model finds, in the opposite direction.

Lyon leads Ashwin on total value and on rate, and there is a checkable reason. Ashwin bowls a lot in India, where this model's country data says spin is easier than almost anywhere measured (factor 0.93). Lyon bowls most of his cricket in Australia, the hardest country for a spinner of any measured (factor 1.19, just ahead of New Zealand's 1.17). Two spinners with similar reputations spent their careers in almost opposite conditions, and once that is priced in it isn't close. Lyon is the best spinner in this dataset at succeeding on pitches that are historically brutal for spin.

What separates the totals is mostly longevity. Anderson has 22 seasons in this dataset, debuting at 20 and still taking the new ball at 41, and only three of them (2005, 2007 and 2019) are below replacement level. His four best seasons (2017, 2018, 2021 and 2022) all came after his 34th birthday, and the single best year of his career arrived at 39. Most fast bowlers are in visible decline a decade before that. Steyn's body didn't get the chance to age the same way, and his injured farewell Tests were below replacement level, so the model is right to count them.

One pattern is flagged and unfixed. Steyn took a higher share of his wickets against the tail than Anderson did (40.6% to 34.0%), and the model can't yet price a wicket differently depending on who it was.

Two more things worth knowing

The model spotted a doctored pitch

R Ashwin's 12 for 98 at Nagpur in 2015 came on a pitch controversial enough that the ICC formally investigated it for excessive turn. A pitch-conditions adjustment should catch that, and it did. Once the model accounted for how helpful the surface was to spin, it marked his figures down sharply. It didn't know the pitch had made headlines. It measured how a replacement spinner would have fared on the same surface and reached the conclusion cricket journalism did at the time.

Big moments count for more

Ben Stokes' unbeaten 135 at Headingley in 2019, with England chasing 359 and the last pair at the crease, is worth noticeably more here than a similar score in a decided match. The model weighs every performance by what was at stake when it happened. That is a large part of why Stokes, who has a habit of producing his best cricket in the tensest moments, rates above what a flat runs-based average would say.

Where it still falls short

Anything before 2001 barely counts

Cricsheet's coverage starts in 2001, so anyone whose career peaked earlier is badly undercounted. Kallis is the clear case in this piece.

Coverage isn't complete until 2009

The 2001 start date understates the problem. Checked against real Test totals for each year, Cricsheet's ball-by-ball data alone captures only 176 of the 377 Tests played between 2001 and 2008. That is under half, and the worst years are 2001 and 2002, with 1 match captured out of 55 and 2 out of 54.

The other 201 are now added from ESPNcricinfo's scorecards. They carry no ball-by-ball detail, so no batting-position adjustment, though leverage is now approximated from the scorecards' own Fall-of-Wickets listings. On the 176 Tests both sources cover, they agree with Cricsheet almost exactly, within a few tenths of a percent on runs, dismissals, wickets, balls bowled and runs conceded. Scored the same way as the rest of the model (pitch, host-country and opposition factors, all rebuilt from the scorecards themselves) and calibrated against the shared matches, player-match value from this coarser method correlates 0.91 with the full model. A player or season marked † anywhere in this piece gets some or all of its value from this layer rather than ball-by-ball data; the full validation, including what happens with each factor added or removed, is in Measure Twice.

Widening the population also gives 2001 to 2008 their own replacement level, computed from these scorecard-included years rather than borrowed from later ones. The fuller mechanics are in Measure Twice.

Anyone whose career ran mostly through 2001–2008 was undercounted too, not only players who retired before 2001. Shane Warne is the clearest example. He has 61 captured Tests now, 37 of them from the scorecard layer, up from 24 on ball-by-ball data alone. That's still short of the six more years he played after the window opens, but a much fuller picture than before. Seasons before 2012 still lean on a replacement level pooled across 2009 onward wherever even the widened data is too thin on its own, so rankings for careers that ran through 2001 to 2011 carry more uncertainty than the decimals suggest.

One setting still moves the top

A replacement player here is anyone with 10 or fewer career Tests, the value used from the start. Moving that cut-off to 8, 12 or 15 keeps Root first every time. Second place is more sensitive than the published table suggests. Lyon takes it at 8, 12 and 15, and only at the published cut-off of 10 does Anderson edge back ahead, by a margin that doesn't survive rounding. Smith moves into third at 12 and 15. Held-out match-outcome accuracy barely moves across any of these settings, 0.981 to 0.982, so the data can't choose between them on predictive grounds, and 10 was never picked to favour a particular ranking. It's simply the one setting, of the four checked, where Anderson comes out ahead of Lyon rather than behind.

Not every team gets the same number of chances to build one

Baseball and basketball teams play the same number of games a season, so a season's WAR total already compares players on level footing. Test cricket has no such luxury, because teams play very different volumes of cricket. Career WAR rewarding a long, consistently good career is what a value stat should do, and I don't treat it as a flaw. The opportunity to build that career isn't shared evenly, though. Root has played 166 Tests to Kane Williamson's 110, and much of that gap is national scheduling. England have played 275 Tests in this window and New Zealand 169, which is 39% fewer. Williamson's WAR per game (0.097 in the Fab Four table, batting only) is essentially tied with Root's (0.099), but his team plays less, so he never had the same chances to add to a career total. The same logic quietly favours the stars of England, Australia and India over those of Sri Lanka, New Zealand and the West Indies, whoever is better.

Career WAR measures how much value a player produced. WAR per game measures it per chance, which is the fairer lens when the question is about ability. A long career spent producing at a high level, like Anderson's or Root's, is real value and deserves credit. A shorter one isn't a lesser career because a team's calendar didn't supply the games. Williamson got 39% fewer chances to show a rate nearly identical to Root's.

Fielding isn't in here at all

I looked at adding it and stopped. Cricsheet records who took a catch but not who dropped one, so there is no way to tell a player who was never given a chance from one who put down every chance. Ground fielding (the stops, the saved boundaries, the turned twos) is invisible unless it ends in a run out. Ravindra Jadeja, whose fielding reputation rests almost entirely on things this dataset can't see, came out worse than average when I checked him against a forced, catches-only fielding WAR: 0.43 catches per Test, 137th of the 193 players with 30 or more Tests. Leaving fielding out and saying so is the better outcome.

The replacement bar is wide, not a single line

Bangladesh and Zimbabwe bowlers dominate the bottom of the career table because the model measures against a global replacement level, not each team's own bar. That is working as intended.

Wicketkeepers are judged on batting alone

The model can't see why a wicketkeeper was picked. A keeper-batter's value is mostly in the gloves, which is invisible here, leaving only a modest batting record.

What I'd fix next

  • Team scheduling bias compounds the replacement-level issue above. The replacement bar is global rather than team-relative, and which teams get to accumulate value at all is uneven too, since the ICC's Full Members play very different volumes of Test cricket against each other. I don't think changing the formula fixes this. A team that plays less cricket has fewer chances to add to a career total, and that is real, not an error. WAR per game is the answer to it, not a correction to career WAR.
  • The country-conditions factor is coarse, and I tested whether that matters. It is one number per country per bowling type per five-year era, which misses variation between grounds within a country. Sydney's spin wicket rate runs above Australia's average, on a sample big enough to trust (25 Tests). I built a venue-level version and it made held-out prediction worse. Sydney's extra turn isn't there from ball one. It is almost entirely a 4th-innings effect, and a venue-level factor smears it across the whole match. Country is still the right grain, for a tested reason rather than a guess about sample size. Full story in Measure Twice.
  • Batting position deserves the same scrutiny wicket pricing just got. Separating how much easier a slot is from which players get sent there needs doing properly. I tried it once and the result didn't hold up well enough to trust, so it isn't reflected here.
  • The model doesn't know who got dismissed, and I tested fixing that too. A wicket is priced the same whether it is a recognised top-order batter or a number 11 swinging for the boundary. That is part of why Steyn's heavier share of tail wickets than Anderson's (40.6% to 34.0%) doesn't show up in either man's number. I built a version that priced each wicket by the dismissed batter's own quality. The first attempt had a real bug that inflated almost every bowler's number, and the corrected version predicted real match outcomes no better than a flat price. The signal is probably real but too weak to earn its place. Full story in Measure Twice.
  • An allrounder's value to a team's balance probably isn't fully captured. Summing batting and bowling WAR should already measure how much better a player is in his slot than a replacement, but only if a real replacement could cover both jobs as easily as the model assumes. I ran four checks on a handful of recognised allrounders. One showed a positive effect for Jadeja and mixed results for the rest, one turned out to be a bowling-type artifact, adding an allrounder indicator to the match model changed nothing, and pricing the credit directly as the value of the roster slot an allrounder frees up lost across a full sweep of how that credit could be sized. That isn't enough to build a fix from, and it isn't proof the effect is absent.

None of this changes what a scorecard already shows. Root has scored more Test runs than anyone since 2001, Anderson has taken more wickets than any other fast bowler in history, and Lyon is one of the most prolific spinners the format has seen. WAR weighs what those numbers meant, against who was faced, what the pitch was doing and what was at stake. On that measure Root and Anderson finish first and second, helped by long careers for a team that plays more Test cricket than most. Lyon is third with far fewer Tests behind him, which suggests he is underrated.
