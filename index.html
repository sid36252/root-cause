Root Cause

What 25 years of Test cricket looks like through Wins Above Replacement

Basketball has Wins Above Replacement. Test cricket doesn't, so I built one from 25 years of ball-by-ball data.

Coverage 2001–2026 Matches 914 Deliveries 1,762,524 Source Cricsheet

In short

  • Built Wins Above Replacement for Test cricket from 1.76m ball-by-ball deliveries, the closest thing this sport has to basketball's WAR.
  • Final leaderboard: Joe Root leads it, outright, ahead of Nathan Lyon, Steve Smith, and everyone else who's played Test cricket since 2001.
  • Batting leads bowling in total value produced, 64.7% to 35.3% — not a split the model was built to hit, just where a fair price for a wicket happens to land. It holds up on a direct test against real match outcomes too.
  • It can't see anything before 2001, or fielding at all — more on both below.

WAR (Wins Above Replacement) is the closest thing basketball and baseball have to a single number that answers "how good is this player, really?" It doesn't ask how many points or runs someone produced. It asks how many wins they added for their team, above what a freely-available replacement-level player would have added in the same games. Test cricket has never had a real version of this. Batting average and bowling average get you partway there, but they don't know who you were facing, what the pitch was doing, or whether the moment mattered.

So I built one. What follows is what it found, using the model as it stands today. There's a companion piece, Measure Twice, covering how it was built and tested, for anyone who wants that instead.

The idea, in plain terms

Imagine every Test team keeps a replacement player in reserve: not a star, just a solid domestic pro who'd slot in if someone got dropped. WAR asks one question: how many extra wins did this player earn their team, above what that replacement would have earned in the same matches?

Everything else is just being careful about what "the same matches" means:

  • Who you faced. A hundred against a full-strength attack should count for more than a hundred against a weak one.
  • What the pitch was doing. Runs on a green seamer are harder to come by than runs on a flat road, and wickets on a raging turner are partly the surface's doing, not just the bowler.
  • What a wicket is actually worth. Not a textbook bowling average, but the real, measured effect losing a batter has on a team's chances of winning.
  • Whether the moment mattered. A wicket that breaks a nervy run chase is worth more than the same wicket in a match already decided.
  • Turning all of that into wins, using the historical relationship between how big a team's run advantage was and how often that actually produced a win.

Is the data any good?

Everything here comes from Cricsheet, a free ball-by-ball archive of international cricket. Test coverage starts in 2001 and runs to the present: 914 matches, 1.76 million individual deliveries, each one carrying the batter, the bowler, the runs, and the dismissal if there was one.

Before trusting any of it, I ran it against known history. Alastair Cook's career is closed. He retired in 2018, so his total is fixed forever, and summing his raw runs straight off the ball-by-ball data landed exactly on his real career tally of 12,472. The dismissal mix checked out too: 58.8% caught, 17.6% bowled, 16.8% lbw, the rest split across run outs, stumpings and oddities. And the era shows up honestly in the numbers: the draw rate has fallen from 24.4% of matches in 2000–04 to just 7.0% in 2025–26. Part of that is the well-documented shift toward more attacking Test cricket. Part of it, in the last five years especially, is the World Test Championship: bilateral series now feed a points table, and a draw is worth a lot less on that table than a win.

The leaderboard

Every adjustment applied at once, all measured in Runs Above Replacement (RAR, the raw currency that gets converted into wins at the very end): opposition quality, pitch difficulty measured separately for scoring rate and wicket-taking rate, country-specific conditions, batting position (all eleven individual slots), a wicket priced at its actual measured win-probability value rather than a textbook average, leverage weighting, season-level win conversion.

Bumrah's 2024 tops the whole dataset by a clear margin — 2.55 WAR against Harbhajan's 2.39 in second place. The underlying season is one of the more remarkable in the dataset's whole 2001–2026 window by raw numbers alone: 71 wickets at an average of 14.93 across 13 Tests, spread over five different away and home assignments in a single calendar year — South Africa in January, then home series against England, Bangladesh and New Zealand (the last of those India's shock 0–3 home whitewash, a series most of India's other bowlers had miserable figures in), before closing the year in Australia for the Border-Gavaskar series. What makes the model's own read on it interesting is what's not driving the number: opposition quality across those 13 Tests averaged almost exactly the dataset norm (0.999, against 1.0 for a perfectly average year of opponents), and leverage came in fractionally below average too (0.953×). This isn't a case of the model finding a soft year against weak sides in high-stakes moments; it's 13 Tests of essentially unadjusted, era-typical difficulty, with career-best output layered on top of it. The one dip in an otherwise dominant year came in the final match, the Boxing Day Test at the MCG (bowling value of −39.6 runs above replacement, the only negative match of his season) — consistent with the back spasm he picked up during that Test, which would go on to rule him out of the series-deciding match in Sydney.

This list is a genuine mix of bat and ball — six of the fifteen are batting seasons. Michael Clarke's 2012 doesn't quite crack it, ranking #22 overall and 13th among pure-batting seasons, despite being one of the most dominant individual years in the sport's history by reputation — a 106.33 average and four double-centuries in a single calendar year, including an unbeaten 329 against India, a feat not even Don Bradman managed. Not for lack of runs or soft conditions either — his opposition and pitch factors that year were close to neutral. Two things explain the gap instead: leverage, since nearly two-thirds of his season's value came from three innings that all landed in matches already decided or heading for a draw (0.86× leverage against a season mean of 1.01×); and the era, since the model's runs-per-win rate for 2010–14 (632) sits well above the 513–520 that eleven of the other fourteen top-15 seasons were earned against. A standout knock simply buys more WAR today than it did in 2012.

A few arguments this settles, sort of

Every era of Test cricket has its pub arguments. A few of this one's biggest hold up cleanly enough in the numbers to be worth a look, though most don't come out quite as tidy as either side of the argument would like.

Lyon vs Ashwin

Lyon sitting at #2 on the overall leaderboard, above every specialist batter bar Root and above bowlers like Anderson who get talked about as the greats of the era, might come as a surprise on its own. He's never really been discussed in the company of Warne or Murali the way this model now places him — a very good, honest, high-volume off-spinner by most reputations, not an all-time-great one.

Lyon leads Ashwin on both total value and rate, though, for a specific, checkable reason, and it's the same reason the model rates him this highly overall. Ashwin's numbers are excellent partly because he bowls a lot in India, where this model's own country-conditions data says spin is easier than almost anywhere else measured (factor 0.91). Lyon's are excellent despite bowling most of his cricket in Australia, which the same data says is the single hardest country for a spinner in world cricket (factor 1.26, well clear of anywhere else). Two spinners with outwardly similar reputations spent their careers in almost opposite conditions, and once that's priced in, it isn't close: Lyon is the best spinner this dataset has seen at succeeding on pitches that are historically brutal for spin.

To be precise about what this table can and can't settle: this is the Fab Four debate in Tests specifically, not across every format. Kohli's case in particular leans heavily on white-ball numbers this can't speak to at all. Within Tests, Root and Smith sit a clear tier above Kohli and Williamson on career batting value — but that's mostly a function of Tests played, and it doesn't hold on rate. Root leads by a wide margin on career batting value, but most of that gap is simply opportunity. The two debuted within two years of each other, at almost identical ages (Root actually the older debutant, at 21 years 11 months to Smith's 21 years 1 month). Smith, though, spent his first two seasons in and out of the side as a legspin-bowling allrounder, playing just 5 Tests in 2010–12 before establishing himself as a specialist batter from 2013. Root, picked as a batter from day one, had 64 Tests banked within five years of his debut to Smith's 38. Add a full 12-month ban from the 2018 ball-tampering scandal, landing in what should have been his peak years, and a busier England calendar (13.0 Tests a year to Australia's 10.4), and most of the remaining 43-Test gap explains itself without either player's batting coming into it. Per Test, the picture flips: Smith's WAR per game (0.113) edges Root's (0.098). Take the career total and Root is the clear answer; take value per match, and Smith comes out ahead. One hypothetical, heavily caveated: hold Smith's rate constant over Root's 166 Tests instead of his own 123, and he finishes with 18.7 batting WAR to Root's real 16.35. Not a fair comparison, since that rate was never actually tested across those extra Tests, but a useful sense of how large the opportunity gap was.

This is close enough now to go either way. Stokes leads on total career value (9.54 to 9.34, in 32 more Tests); Jadeja leads clearly on WAR per game (0.105 to 0.079, 33% higher) in a shorter one. The shape of each score is the more interesting story. For most of his career Jadeja was a bowler who could bat a bit, not a real second threat: his batting average sat at 31.5 through 2018, only catching up recently (42.4 since 2019, now good enough to be picked on runs alone). Even so, his value here is still overwhelmingly bowling-led, and like Ashwin above, a lot of that bowling comes in conditions this model rates as unusually kind to spin. Stokes' batting and bowling have stayed closer to level for most of his career, a rarer shape than a fine bowler whose batting caught up late. Who produced more across a career: Stokes, narrowly. Who's been more valuable per match: Jadeja, clearly.

One more angle on Stokes: some of that value is specifically match-winning, in a sense this model can actually check. Every performance here is leverage-weighted by how much was at stake when it happened, and stripping that weighting back out shows what each player would be worth on raw production alone. Take it out and Stokes' total drops by 5.1%, meaning leverage is a net positive for him — his output skews toward the moments that mattered. Take it out for Jadeja and his total rises by 17.7%, the opposite pattern: a meaningful share of his production came in matches that were already largely decided. That's Stokes' clutch reputation showing up as an actual number, not just a talking point.

Shakib Al Hasan belongs in this conversation too, even though he isn't rated as highly as either of these two in Test cricket specifically. A genuine two-way contributor like Stokes rather than a specialist like Jadeja, his WAR per game still edges Stokes' — but he falls short of both on total value, mostly for lack of Tests played (68, against Jadeja's 89 and Stokes' 121).

Jacques Kallis, the allrounder both of these two inevitably get measured against, barely registers here for two different reasons. The batting is mostly a data problem: this dataset only captures 89 of his 166 Tests, missing the first six years of an 18-year career that began in 1995, before Cricsheet's coverage starts, and what's captured is a fair cross-section of his prime (2007, 2010 and 2012 all rate among his best), so a full career would likely rate well above the 4.76 WAR shown. The bowling is a different case, and possibly a reputation problem rather than a data one: his wicket-taking record is genuinely good enough that he's often talked about as a near-frontline third seamer, not just a support option, but that reading may be doing some work it shouldn't. Nearly half of his captured bowling (49% of the balls) came in South Africa, which this model rates as one of the easier countries in the world for pace bowling (factor 0.88, the same discount applied to Steyn above), and home advantage on top of that. Once conditions and opposition are priced in the way they are for every other bowler here, it nets out to essentially zero across his captured career, mostly in low-leverage overs — a support seamer who filled overs and chipped in on helpful days, not a strike weapon. Judged against replacement level, that's exactly where a role like that should land.

The model doesn't just prefer Anderson here — it isn't especially close, more than double on total value (13.01 to 5.29), and ahead on WAR per game too. That's the hardest verdict in this piece to take at face value, because by the numbers most judges actually look at, Steyn's the better bowler: a bowling average of 22.95 against 26.45, at a faster strike rate, over a career many rate as the more fearsome of the two at its absolute peak.

Part of that is the same mechanism that discounted Ashwin and Jadeja: Steyn took 59% of his wickets in South Africa, which this model rates as one of the easier countries for pace bowling (factor 0.88), against Anderson's 65% in a much less forgiving England (0.98).

There's a cleaner explanation that doesn't need that caveat at all. Split Steyn's career at the end of his last fully fit season and the gap nearly disappears: 82 Tests of prime Steyn (2006–2016) produced 0.0722 bowling WAR per Test, within 1% of Anderson's whole-career rate of 0.0730. His last 8 Tests, played after multiple shoulder and heel surgeries, were actively bad (−0.80 WAR), and that alone drags his career figure down to 5.29. At full health, this model rates them as the same bowler.

What actually separates the final numbers, then, isn't peak quality — it's that Anderson's longevity is one of the most remarkable in the sport's history, and it deserves to be said plainly rather than just implied by a number. Twenty-one seasons in this dataset, debuting at 20 and still taking the new ball at 41, with only four of them landing below replacement level, mostly early, growing-pains years. Three of his four best seasons by this model's reckoning — 2017, 2021, and 2022 — all came after his 34th birthday, and the single best year of his entire career arrived at 39, well clear of anything he produced in his twenties or early thirties. Most fast bowlers are in visible decline a decade before that; Anderson somehow kept getting better. Steyn's body didn't get the chance to age the same way; his injured farewell Tests really were below replacement level, and the model is right to count them.

One flagged, unfixed pattern: Steyn took a higher share of his wickets against the tail than Anderson did (40.6% to 34.0%), and this model can't yet price a wicket differently depending on who it was.

Two more things worth knowing

The model spotted a doctored pitch

R Ashwin's 12 wickets for 98 at Nagpur in 2015, on a pitch controversial enough that the ICC formally investigated it for excessive turn, is exactly the case a pitch-conditions adjustment should catch. Once the model accounted for how helpful that specific surface was to spin, his bowling value for that match dropped from 0.71 WAR to 0.31 — more than halved. It didn't know the pitch had made headlines. It just measured how a replacement spinner would have fared on the same surface, and reached the same conclusion cricket journalism did at the time.

Big moments count for more

Ben Stokes' unbeaten 135 at Headingley in 2019, England chasing 359 with the last pair at the crease, is worth noticeably more here than a similar score in a match already decided. The model weighs every performance by how much was genuinely at stake when it happened, which is a large part of why a player like Stokes, who has a habit of producing his best cricket in the tensest moments, rates well above what a flat runs-based average would say.

Where it still falls short

Anything before 2001 barely counts

Cricsheet's ball-by-ball coverage starts in 2001, so anyone whose career peaked earlier gets badly undercounted. Jacques Kallis, above, is the clearest case in this piece, but he's far from alone: any bowler or batter whose best years fell in the 1990s is judged here on whatever was left of their career after 2001, not on their true peak.

Not every team gets the same number of chances to build one

Career WAR rewarding a long, consistently good career isn't a shortcoming to apologise for — that's exactly what a value-produced stat should do, the same way it works in baseball or basketball. What's worth flagging plainly, though, is that the chance to produce that volume isn't evenly shared. Root has played 166 Tests to Kane Williamson's 110, and a good chunk of that gap is national scheduling, not application or fitness: England have played 275 Tests in this window, New Zealand 169, 39% fewer. Williamson's WAR per game (0.097 in the Fab Four table above, batting only) is essentially tied with Root's (0.098), but he was never going to get the same number of chances to add to a career total, because his team simply doesn't play as much cricket. The same logic quietly favours England, Australia and India's stars over Sri Lanka's, New Zealand's and the West Indies', independent of who's actually better. Career WAR still honestly answers "how much value did you produce"; WAR per game answers "how much value per chance you got," which is the fairer lens when the question is really about ability rather than opportunity. The principle underneath both numbers is the same: a long career spent producing at a high level, like Anderson's or Root's, is real value and deserves to be credited as such, not treated as some lesser feat than a short, brilliant one. But a shorter one shouldn't be read as a lesser player just because their team's calendar never gave them the games to build the same total — Williamson isn't a smaller version of Root, he's a player with an almost identical rate who got 39% fewer chances to show it. Longevity earns its credit; a lack of opportunity shouldn't cost anyone theirs.

Fielding isn't in here at all

I looked hard at adding it, and stopped. Cricsheet records who took a catch, but not who dropped one, so there's no way to distinguish a player who was never given a chance from one who put down every chance they got. Ground fielding (the stops, the saved boundaries, the turned twos) is invisible entirely unless it happens to end in a run out. A player like Ravindra Jadeja, whose fielding reputation rests almost entirely on things this dataset can't see, would come out of a forced "fielding WAR" looking ordinary, which would be a worse outcome than leaving it out and saying so.

The replacement bar is wide, not a single line

Bangladesh and Zimbabwe bowlers dominate the bottom of the career table, and that's not a bug. It's the model correctly measuring performance against a global replacement level rather than each team's own bar. And the model has no way to see why a wicketkeeper was picked: a keeper-batter's real value is mostly in the gloves, and everything about that is invisible here, leaving only a modest batting record behind.

What I'd fix next

  • Team scheduling bias compounds the replacement-level issue above. Not only is the replacement bar global rather than team-relative, but which teams get to accumulate value at all is itself uneven, since the ICC's Full Members don't play remotely similar volumes of Test cricket against each other. I don't think this is fixable by changing the RAR formula — a team that plays less cricket genuinely has fewer chances to add to a career total, and that's real, not an error. WAR per game is the honest answer to it, not a correction to career WAR itself.
  • The country-conditions factor is still fairly coarse, and I actually tested whether that matters. It's one number per country per bowling type per five-year era, which misses real variation between specific grounds within a country: Sydney's spin wicket rate genuinely does run above Australia's average, and the Gabba's runs below it, on samples big enough to trust (14–25 Tests each). Built a venue-level version anyway, and it made the model worse, not better, on held-out prediction. The reason turned out to be more interesting than "not enough data": Sydney's extra turn isn't there from ball one, it's almost entirely a 4th-innings effect, and a factor built at the venue level smears that flat across a whole match. Country, not venue, is still the right grain, now for a specific, tested reason rather than a guess about sample size. Full story in Measure Twice.
  • Batting position deserves the same scrutiny wicket pricing just got. Separating how much easier a slot is from which players get sent there is worth doing properly. I've tried it once and the result didn't hold up well enough to trust, so it isn't reflected here yet.
  • Bowling RAR doesn't know who got dismissed, and I tested fixing that too. A wicket is priced the same whether it's a recognised top-order batter or a number 11 swinging for the boundary, part of why Steyn's heavier share of tail wickets than Anderson's (40.6% to 34.0%) doesn't show up anywhere in either man's number. Built a version that priced each wicket by the dismissed batter's own quality, found and fixed a real bug in the first attempt (it was inflating almost every bowler's number instead of redistributing credit), and even the corrected version didn't predict real match outcomes any better than pricing every wicket the same. The signal is probably real but too weak, at this level of accuracy, to earn its place. Full story in Measure Twice.
  • An allrounder's value to a team's overall balance probably isn't fully captured. Summing batting and bowling WAR for one player should, in principle, already measure how much better this player is in this slot than a replacement — but only if a real replacement could cover both jobs as easily as the model assumes. I've checked this for a handful of recognised allrounders and found a clean effect for one of them and nothing consistent for the rest, which isn't enough to build a fix from yet. It still feels like a gap rather than a closed question.
